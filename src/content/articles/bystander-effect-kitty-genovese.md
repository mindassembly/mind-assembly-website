## What changed about the story?

Catherine “Kitty” Genovese was murdered in Queens in 1964. A widely repeated account claimed that 38 witnesses watched without intervening. Manning and colleagues (2007) examined archival material and found that it failed to support the number, the claim that witnesses saw the murder or the claim that everyone remained inactive. The story became a textbook illustration of how people behave in groups even though the historical evidence could not establish that account.

## What did the experiments show?

Darley and Latané (1968) staged an apparent seizure over an intercom. Participants who believed other listeners were present were generally slower or less likely to report the emergency. One explanation is **diffusion of responsibility**. When several people could act, each may feel less personally responsible. A later meta-analysis found an average bystander effect that varied by context and was weaker in some dangerous situations (Fischer et al., 2011).

## Can a crowd make help more likely?

Philpot and colleagues (2020) examined footage of 219 public conflicts across three countries. In about nine out of ten incidents, at least one person intervened. This observation concerns the chance of **anyone in the group** helping. The laboratory question often concerns the chance of **one particular person** helping. A crowd can offer more potential helpers even if each individual is less likely to act. The video study covers observed public conflicts, so its estimate should stay tied to that setting.

When reading a claim about helping, check whose behaviour was counted and what they could see, hear or reasonably infer about the situation.
