## What happened in the simulated prison?

In 1971, researchers randomly assigned screened male volunteers to play guards or prisoners in a simulated prison at Stanford. The study was scheduled to run for two weeks and ended after six days. The original report described escalating mistreatment by some guards and distress among prisoners. It attributed much of what happened to the social forces created by the prison setting (Haney et al., 1973).

## What did the archive change?

Le Texier (2019) examined study records and interviewed participants. He documented detailed instructions given to guards about how to run the prison and treat prisoners, as well as gaps in the collection of observations. The researchers helped create and operate the institution they were studying. Guard behaviour also varied, which makes an account of uniform role conformity hard to sustain.

Random assignment helped address whether volunteers chose the roles that suited them. It left a different problem unresolved. The guard role came bundled with authority, instructions and cues about what the researchers wanted. The design gives little basis for estimating how much each element contributed.

## What can we take from it?

The study records distress and mistreatment in one simulation. A stronger test of spontaneous role conformity would compare groups given the same authority with different instructions and would specify in advance how behaviour would be recorded. The case also raises a question about oversight. Who can stop a study when the person responsible for participant welfare is also invested in keeping the simulation running?
