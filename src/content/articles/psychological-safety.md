## What makes speaking up feel risky?

Imagine noticing that your project group has misread a study. You could ask everyone to check the paper again, but you might worry about looking uninformed or irritating the person who led the discussion. **Team psychological safety** is the shared expectation that people can take interpersonal risks like these without being humiliated or penalised for speaking up (Edmondson, 1999).

The risk depends partly on the setting. A student might readily admit confusion to one project group and stay quiet in another. The same person faces different expectations about how the group will respond.

## What might it look like in a student group?

Suppose someone says, “I’m unsure this paper supports our conclusion.” A useful response would be, “Which part makes you unsure? Let’s look at it together.” The group can still debate the interpretation and expect everyone to bring evidence. How the group handles the question helps members judge whether to raise the next one.

One friendly response gives people a reason to try again. Over time, members also notice whose concerns get taken seriously and whether people with less authority can disagree openly. Silence alone is ambiguous. It could reflect agreement, uncertainty, time pressure or fear of consequences.

## Try this in a reading discussion

Before agreeing on a summary, ask each person to name one point they are unsure about. Give people a moment to think and let them pass. When someone raises a concern, ask what evidence would clarify it and check that evidence together. At the next meeting, say what the group changed after considering the concern.

This is a discussion exercise informed by workplace research. Its impact in a specific student group would need to be evaluated.
