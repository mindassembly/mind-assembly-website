## Read for decisions

1. **Design.** What was observed or manipulated? When were variables measured? Identify the comparison and whether participants were randomly assigned.
2. **Sample.** Where did recruitment happen? Track who qualified, enrolled, dropped out or was excluded. Compare the final analytic sample with the group the paper claims to describe.
3. **Measures.** Find out how each construct became a score or observation. Check whether evidence supports using that measure with these participants and for this purpose.
4. **Procedure.** Follow what participants experienced in order. Look for differences between groups beyond the intended manipulation, and check whether assessors knew the group assignments when that matters.
5. **Analysis.** Identify the primary outcome, main comparison and how missing data were handled. Consult the supplement or preregistration when important decisions are unclear.

## Try it on a claim

A paper reports that students who slept longer scored higher on an attention task. Ask whether sleep was assigned or reported, when it was measured and what else might explain the difference. If this was a one-time survey with self-reported sleep, the conclusion should describe an association in that sample. The Methods section tells you which conclusions the design can support.

The APA reporting standards are a useful checklist for quantitative papers. Treat missing details as questions to investigate in the appendix, protocol or data documentation.
