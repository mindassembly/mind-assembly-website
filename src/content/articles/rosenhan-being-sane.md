## What Rosenhan reported

In his 1973 paper, David Rosenhan reported that eight people sought admission to psychiatric hospitals by claiming to hear voices. According to the paper, they stopped simulating the presenting symptom after admission. Rosenhan argued that staff interpreted ordinary behaviour through the lens of a psychiatric diagnosis. In a second exercise, he reported that a hospital expecting pseudopatients suspected some genuine admissions, although his team had sent nobody.

## What did the design measure?

Robert Spitzer (1975) challenged the leap from clinicians accepting a reported symptom to a claim about the validity of psychiatric diagnosis. A clinician has to make decisions with the information available at intake, including what a patient reports. Detecting deliberate simulation, agreement between clinicians and accuracy of diagnosis are three different questions. The study provides no representative estimate of how often ordinary patients are misdiagnosed.

## What did later investigators find?

Susannah Cahalan’s reporting raised questions about the consistency of the published account with Rosenhan’s own hospital record and about how many of the reported participants could be independently identified. Andrew Scull (2023), drawing on records Cahalan shared, argued that the discrepancies amounted to scientific fraud. That is Scull’s assessment of the evidence. The incomplete record limits what can now be verified about individual admissions.

The enduring clinical question is how a label shapes the way staff interpret behaviour. To investigate that effect directly, a study could show clinicians identical case descriptions while varying only the prior diagnosis, then compare how they interpret the same behaviour.
