## The central idea

In psychodynamic traditions, a **defence mechanism** refers to a process that protects a person from psychological conflict, anxiety, or distress. Classical accounts emphasise that these processes are often outside conscious awareness. The concept belongs to a theoretical tradition; identifying a defence in someone's behaviour is an interpretation, not a laboratory finding.

## Some important distinctions

- **Denial** involves difficulty acknowledging information that is psychologically threatening.
- **Projection** describes attributing one's own unacceptable feelings or impulses to someone else.
- **Displacement** describes redirecting an emotional response from one target toward another.
- **Sublimation** describes redirecting impulses into an activity that is more acceptable or constructive.

These are simplified descriptions. In clinical work, context, patterns over time, and the person's own account matter much more than a single behaviour.

## Why the concept remains useful

Defence language can help organise a hypothesis about how someone manages conflict. It can also encourage reflection on what a behaviour *does* for a person, rather than only what it looks like. It becomes misleading when a defence is treated as an objective label that can be assigned after a short interaction.

## A question worth holding

If someone avoids a distressing subject, are they defending against painful feelings—or are they choosing not to disclose something? Both are possible. A responsible interpretation requires evidence and humility.
