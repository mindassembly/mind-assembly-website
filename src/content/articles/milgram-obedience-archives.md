## What does the 65% refer to?

In the condition reported by Stanley Milgram in 1963, 26 of 40 participants continued to the highest setting marked 450 volts. Participants played a teacher in a supposed learning experiment. They were told to deliver increasingly strong shocks when a learner made errors. The learner was working with the researchers and received no shocks. The 65% figure describes one condition, and the voltage records the switch selected during that procedure.

## What did recordings reveal?

Gibson (2013) analysed exchanges between the experimenter and participants in archival recordings and transcripts. Participants questioned what was happening and disputed whether they should continue. The experimenter’s attempts to secure cooperation could involve argument and persuasion. These interactions make the process harder to describe with a simple count of who followed a fixed sequence of instructions.

## Did participants all believe the same thing?

Perry and colleagues (2020) reanalysed an unpublished study of participants’ belief in the procedure. Belief varied, and their analysis found that participants who believed the shocks were painful tended to stop earlier across several conditions. These are associations based on retrospective reports and archival records. A participant’s later account cannot fully reveal what they believed at each step.

The archive adds two questions to the familiar result. What did each participant think the learner was experiencing? What did the experimenter say when that participant tried to stop? Those questions help distinguish persuasion, trust in the research setting and submission to authority as possible explanations for continuing.
