## How a result gets selected

Suppose a team tests several outcomes and exclusion rules, then reports only the combination that produced a small p-value. This is an example of p-hacking. The reader sees one test while the process gave chance several opportunities to produce an apparently striking result.

Publication bias can operate across entire studies. If projects yielding clear or positive results are more likely to be written up or published, a literature search will give a distorted picture of what researchers found. Franco and colleagues studied this process by tracking a known set of completed social science studies.

## What preregistration changes

A preregistration records decisions such as hypotheses, outcomes, exclusions, sample size and analyses before the relevant results are known. Compare that plan with the paper to check what was prespecified and what changed. Exploratory work can generate new hypotheses, and transparent reporting lets readers judge how those hypotheses arose.

Preregistration has limits. An imprecise plan leaves substantial flexibility, and a precisely registered design can still have weak measures. Check the quality of the plan and the reasons for any deviations.
