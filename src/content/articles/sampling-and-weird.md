## Who does the sample represent?

Start with the population the authors want to describe. Then ask who could actually be recruited, who chose to participate, and who completed the study. A large sample can give a precise estimate for the group reached while still missing people the authors want to make claims about.

Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan (2010) reviewed evidence that behavioural researchers relied heavily on Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic populations. Some of those populations produced unusual results in cross-cultural comparisons. The degree of variation depended on the phenomenon being studied. The label WEIRD describes a sampling problem and can hide important differences within the populations it groups together.

## What would make a conclusion travel?

Imagine studying belonging among first-year students recruited through one English-medium university's clubs. Students outside clubs may have different experiences. A second campus could test whether the finding travels, provided the questions capture comparable experiences at both sites. Random assignment to conditions within the first campus could strengthen a causal comparison there. Recruitment and measurement would still limit who that result can describe.

When reading a paper, compare the population named in its conclusion with the people described in the Methods section.
