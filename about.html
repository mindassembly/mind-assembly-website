About Mind Assembly

An independent, student-led psychology collective.

Mind Assembly is a student-led psychology collective for psychology students, graduates, and anyone curious about how we understand the mind and human behaviour.

We bring people together to explore psychological ideas, engage with research, question established perspectives, and examine the evidence behind what we know. Through shared learning, thoughtful discussions, and collaborative initiatives, we create opportunities to look beyond conventional classroom learning and engage with psychology as an evolving scientific discipline.

We believe that understanding psychology requires more than learning theories and concepts. It involves asking difficult questions, examining different perspectives, recognising the limitations of existing knowledge, and remaining open to new ways of thinking.

At Mind Assembly, curiosity is the starting point, evidence guides the conversation, and learning is something we build together.

Why we exist

A lecture can introduce a theory. A paper can give you a finding. Where do you take the questions they leave you with?

Mind Assembly makes room to work through those questions together. We want to be able to stop at a sentence in a paper, ask how the researchers reached their conclusion, and hear how someone else read it.

Take psychological safety. A useful starting question is what makes it easier to raise a concern in a group. Reading the research gives us another question to discuss: what evidence would tell us why people speak up in one group and stay quiet in another?

Beyond the label

What a definition leaves out.

01

Can a diagnosis tell the whole story?

A diagnosis can give someone a language for their distress and help guide support. But two people with the same diagnosis may have very different histories, relationships and experiences. How much can a label really tell us about the person behind it?

02

Why does a memory feel so certain?

We often trust our memories because they feel vivid and familiar. Yet remembering is not the same as replaying a recording. What happens when our confidence in a memory is greater than its accuracy?

03

Who decides what counts as normal?

A behaviour understood one way in a particular culture, relationship or situation may be interpreted differently somewhere else. How much of what we consider normal depends on the context in which we observe it?

These are the spaces where psychology becomes especially interesting — between an explanation and the human experience it attempts to describe.
